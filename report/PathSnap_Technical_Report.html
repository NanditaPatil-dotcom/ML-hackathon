Missing values. The inventory shows 0 null GPS rows and 0 duplicate rows across the 90 trips. The main data-quality issue is time gaps rather than missing values: most steps are exactly 1 s, but some trips have gaps up to 153064 s (about 43 hours, a recording break inside one trip). Every trip has a reference .route file in these two partitions.

Preprocessing (src/pathsnap/data/preprocessing)

  • Cleaning: drop null, duplicate and out-of-range rows; enforce increasing timestamps; drop points whose implied speed exceeds 62.5 m/s or acceleration exceeds 8 m/s² (clean.py).
  • Distances: geodesic (haversine) distances in metres, never Euclidean distance in degrees (geo.py).
  • Road graph: directed graph from .nodes/.arcs with a KD-tree over segments for nearest-road lookup (road_graph.py).
  • Candidates: every arc within 40 m of a GPS point is a candidate (features.py). Mean candidate miss rate on student_test (reference arc not in the candidate set): 2.3%.
  • Labels: a candidate is positive when it is the .route arc matched to that point (route_match.py).
  • Splits: grouped by trip_id everywhere (5-fold GroupKFold on the 80 training trips).

Features and why

  • dist_to_segment_m: perpendicular distance from the GPS point to the arc; the strongest single signal.
  • bearing_alignment_deg: angle between travel direction and arc direction; separates parallel and opposite-direction roads that are only a few metres apart.
  • point_speed_mps, speed_limit_proxy_mps: observed speed versus the typical speed on that arc.
  • arc_length_m and candidate_rank (rank by distance within the point's candidate set).
  • candidate_arc_id is excluded: arc ids are per-trip row numbers, so a model could only memorise them.

2. Model experimentation and comparison

All five models use the same candidate table and features, are fit on training trips only, and are compared with 5-fold trip-grouped CV. For the comparison, training used 44,898 candidate rows sampled by GPS point from all 80 training trips (RBF SVM cannot be trained on the full 1.15M rows in reasonable time). Student-test numbers are on all 124,324 candidate rows of the 10 student_test trips.

Primary selection metric: lowest mean trip-grouped CV log loss on training trips only. Log loss is used because the classifier's probabilities become the HMM emission probabilities, so calibration matters more than thresholded accuracy. Selected model: CatBoost.

2.2 Per-model questions

CatBoost

A1. Why suitable: Gradient boosting on a small set of numeric, partly non-linear features (distance, angle, speed) is a natural fit; ordered boosting gives well-calibrated probabilities with little tuning.

A2. Strengths and limitations: Strengths: lowest CV log loss, fast inference, robust defaults. Limitations: slower to train than LightGBM; a single global model cannot use sequence context (that is the HMM's job).

B5. Hyperparameters (initial values): iterations=500, depth=6, learning_rate=0.05, loss_function=Logloss (configs/models.yaml).

B6. Loss / objective: Logloss (binary cross-entropy).

C7–8. Student-test performance: top-1 accuracy 77.9%, macro F1 0.865, log loss 0.1373, ROC AUC 0.969, route continuity 87.3%, mean per-trip pointwise top-1 80.0%. Confusion matrix [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]] = [[106235, 3099], [3842, 11148]].

C9. Over/underfitting: No sign of overfitting: student-test log loss is not worse than CV log loss (0.1373 vs 0.1583), and the CV macro F1 spread across folds is small (± 0.019).

LightGBM

A1. Why suitable: Same boosting idea as CatBoost with histogram-based, leaf-wise trees; good for 1M+ rows.

A2. Strengths and limitations: Strengths: fastest training, near-identical accuracy to CatBoost. Limitations: slightly worse log loss; leaf-wise growth can overfit small groups if num_leaves is large.

B5. Hyperparameters (initial values): n_estimators=150, learning_rate=0.08, num_leaves=31, max_depth=6, subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8 (configs/models.yaml).

B6. Loss / objective: binary log loss.

C7–8. Student-test performance: top-1 accuracy 78.1%, macro F1 0.865, log loss 0.1381, ROC AUC 0.969, route continuity 87.4%, mean per-trip pointwise top-1 80.2%. Confusion matrix [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]] = [[106234, 3100], [3873, 11117]].

C9. Over/underfitting: No sign of overfitting: student-test log loss is not worse than CV log loss (0.1381 vs 0.1595), and the CV macro F1 spread across folds is small (± 0.021).

ExtraTrees

A1. Why suitable: Randomised tree ensemble; a low-variance non-linear baseline that needs no feature scaling.

A2. Strengths and limitations: Strengths: quick to train, highest positive-class precision among trees. Limitations: higher log loss (probabilities are vote fractions), large model and slower inference than boosting.

B5. Hyperparameters (initial values): n_estimators=400, max_depth=None, min_samples_split=2, min_samples_leaf=1, max_features=sqrt, class_weight=balanced (configs/models.yaml).

B6. Loss / objective: Gini impurity per split.

C7–8. Student-test performance: top-1 accuracy 77.8%, macro F1 0.860, log loss 0.1421, ROC AUC 0.967, route continuity 87.2%, mean per-trip pointwise top-1 80.0%. Confusion matrix [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]] = [[106771, 2563], [4402, 10588]].

C9. Over/underfitting: No sign of overfitting: student-test log loss is not worse than CV log loss (0.1421 vs 0.1622), and the CV macro F1 spread across folds is small (± 0.017).

RandomForest

A1. Why suitable: Bagged decision trees; standard strong baseline for tabular data.

A2. Strengths and limitations: Strengths: highest positive-class recall (class_weight=balanced_subsample). Limitations: worst tree-model log loss, slower inference, large artifact.

B5. Hyperparameters (initial values): n_estimators=400, max_features=sqrt, min_samples_leaf=2, class_weight=balanced_subsample (configs/models.yaml).

B6. Loss / objective: Gini impurity per split.

C7–8. Student-test performance: top-1 accuracy 77.3%, macro F1 0.864, log loss 0.1515, ROC AUC 0.965, route continuity 86.8%, mean per-trip pointwise top-1 79.5%. Confusion matrix [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]] = [[105348, 3986], [3323, 11667]].

C9. Over/underfitting: No sign of overfitting: student-test log loss is not worse than CV log loss (0.1515 vs 0.1759), and the CV macro F1 spread across folds is small (± 0.020).

RBF_SVM

A1. Why suitable: Kernel method from a different family, to check whether a smooth non-linear boundary does better than trees.

A2. Strengths and limitations: Strengths: competitive top-1 accuracy. Limitations: O(n²) training (only feasible on a sample), probabilities come from Platt scaling and have the worst log loss, and inference is roughly 1000x slower per candidate than CatBoost, which is too slow to run inside the decoder.

B5. Hyperparameters (initial values): kernel=rbf, C=3.0, gamma=scale, probability=True (configs/models.yaml).

B6. Loss / objective: hinge loss with RBF kernel, then Platt-scaled probabilities.

C7–8. Student-test performance: top-1 accuracy 78.3%, macro F1 0.853, log loss 0.1806, ROC AUC 0.932, route continuity 86.5%, mean per-trip pointwise top-1 80.2%. Confusion matrix [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]] = [[107287, 2047], [5031, 9959]].

C9. Over/underfitting: No sign of overfitting: student-test log loss is not worse than CV log loss (0.1806 vs 0.1995), and the CV macro F1 spread across folds is small (± 0.016).

3. Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation (CatBoost)

Method: Optuna TPE (seed 42); trial 0 enqueued as the untuned defaults so the search can only improve on them, trip-grouped CV on the 80 training trips only.

1–2. Search space:

  • iterations: int 200-1000, step 100
  • depth: int 4-10
  • learning_rate: float 0.02-0.3, log scale
  • l2_leaf_reg: float 1-20, log scale
  • bagging_temperature: float 0-3
  • random_strength: float 0.1-5, log scale
  • border_count: categorical {32, 64, 128, 254}

3. Objective: Minimise mean CV log loss, 3-fold trip-grouped GroupKFold on a 60,000-GPS-point subsample of the 80 training trips (448,912 candidate rows). Log loss is used because the HMM consumes the probabilities as emissions..

4. Trials: 25 (full trial log: results/bayesian/).

5. Best values: iterations=300, depth=7, learning_rate=0.02589255471132382, l2_leaf_reg=7.5054639699677805, bagging_temperature=1.7123914730730858, random_strength=2.0742912474922917, border_count=128.

4. HMM and Viterbi route reconstruction (src/pathsnap/decoder)

  1. Hidden states: the candidate arcs of each GPS observation (arc_id, plus where the point projects on it). The candidate-to-arc correspondence is kept, so the decoded state is directly a road segment.
  2. Emissions: CatBoost probability for each candidate, normalised over that point's candidates and floored at 1e-9 (CandidateScorer.emission_log_prob).
  3. Transitions: Newson & Krumm style, in log space: -|route_dist - gps_dist| / beta - turn_weight · turn_deg / 180, where route_dist is the shortest drivable distance (Dijkstra on the directed arcs) between the two candidate positions. beta = 50 m, turn_weight = 3, backtrack tolerance 30 m.
  4. Missing candidates and large GPS gaps: a point with no candidates gets no road (and is counted as wrong in Table 3); gps_dist grows with the gap, so long gaps are not penalised for covering more distance.
  5. Disconnected segments: a transition is impossible if no path exists within 4·gps_dist + 300 m. If every transition at a step is impossible, Viterbi restarts from that point; in the final route the gap is filled with the shortest-path arcs.
  6. Recurrence: δt(j) = maxi[δt−1(i) + log P(j|i)] + log P(ot|j), with back-pointers.
